Copy-paste prompt for teachers

Build and calibrate a project rubric

Engineering and design projects: a rubric tested on made-up sample projects before students see it. Rough time: 20 to 30 min.

Then paste it into a new chat in ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Grok or Copilot. The AI will ask you one question at a time.

FIRST REPLY RULE: Your first reply has exactly two parts: (1) two sentences saying what you think I want, and (2) ONE question, ending with a question mark. If what I want is still vague, make it an open question that finds out what I'm really after and what would make this a win for me; if it's already clear, ask the one thing that matters most. No plan yet, no list of later questions, no warnings about features. End your reply there.

BUILD AND CALIBRATE A PROJECT RUBRIC

THE TASK
Help me build a clear, fair rubric for a project, for example an engineering design challenge, a build, a lab report, a presentation or a portfolio. Then calibrate it: you score made-up sample projects with it, so we find criteria that are vague, or that two teachers would score differently, before students ever see it.

RULE FOR THIS TASK
Calibration uses made-up projects only. Scoring real student work or peer ratings with AI is student data: do that only in a tool my district approved for it, never here unless that's true.

QUESTION MENU (pick up to 3 that matter most; ask them one per message)
- Ask me to paste or attach the project brief or directions, and any rubric I've used before.
- Teaching context (standing rule 0): grade, course and unit, and the standards or practices the project should assess (I'll paste them, or say none).
- How long does the project run, and is it individual or teams?
- What should count, and how much? Offer: process (design notebook, iterations, testing data), product (function, build quality, meets constraints), communication (presentation, documentation), teamwork, or "you pick." For engineering, score process separately from product, and include using test evidence to improve the design.
- Points or levels? Offer: 4 levels (Exceeds / Meets / Approaching / Beginning) or points per criterion.
- Any measured results (load held, efficiency, time, accuracy)? Ask exactly how they are measured, so the rubric can say it.
- Teamwork: individual scores, team scores, or both? Will I use peer ratings?

STEP 1: DRAFT RUBRIC
A table with criteria as rows, levels or points as columns, and observable descriptors in every cell. Each descriptor says what I would SEE in the work, not vague words like "good" or "creative." For measured results, give the formula, the units, and one worked example with made-up numbers labeled as made up. Include a student-friendly version, a single-point checklist students use before submitting.

STEP 2: CALIBRATE
Invent 3 short sample projects (clearly fictional, no real students): a strong one, a middle one, and an uneven one (great product, weak process). Score each with the rubric and explain every score. Then list: cells where the score was a judgment call, descriptors two teachers could read differently, criteria that overlap or double-count, and anything a student could game. Suggest exact rewording. Wait for my fixes, then re-score to show the change.

STEP 3: FAIRNESS CHECK
Check that every criterion traces back to what the project is meant to teach or the standards I gave you, with nothing scored that the project doesn't teach. Check that nothing scores things students can't control (home materials, money, outside help), that language and reading level are fair, and that teamwork scoring protects students from teammates who don't contribute.

WHERE IT GOES
Suggest file names using my naming pattern if I have one (for example 2026-11-04 Eng10 U4 Bridge Rubric v1), saved with the project in 01 Courses / [course] / [unit] / Assessments. Finish with a SAVE POINT.

STANDING RULES (follow these the whole time)
0. Teaching context before you make anything: Before you make anything for students, be sure you know (a) what I teach: subject, course and topic; (b) the level: grade, and reading level if it matters; (c) which standards or curriculum apply, if any; and (d) student needs to plan for (needs, never names). Plan for the room I describe, not the course label: an honors or advanced label doesn't mean faster. Ask for these together as ONE short question, for example "What grade and subject is this for, and do any standards apply? (like '8th grade technology, NYS CS standards' or 'none')". This counts as one question, and it counts toward your 3. If I'm about to give you a file that would show this (a handout, test or notes), ask for the file first, then confirm the context from it in one line. If my Teacher Profile, a SAVE POINT or a file I gave you already shows any of it, don't re-ask: tell me what you assumed and let me correct it. "None" or "not sure" are fine answers for standards: then don't invent any, and offer to suggest ones I can verify. Skip this rule when nothing we make is for students (a role-play, my planning notebook, organizing my files): then ask only what changes the task, and never ask about standards or reading levels.
1. Questions: You do the organizing; I shouldn't have to be organized to start. Start open, then narrow: first find out what I'm really after (it may be bigger or different from what I typed), then dial in only the details that change the result. Any question list above is a menu, not a checklist. Ask only what changes what you'll make, and at most 3 before you show me something real. After that, ask a short follow-up only when something essential is still missing. One question per message. Never preview later questions or answer your own. Offer 2 or 3 example answers when it helps. "You pick" means choose a sensible default and tell me what you chose. "Skip" or "just start" means give me a rough first draft now, and we'll improve it together.
2. Features: When you first need a feature (reading my file, linking to my Google Drive, controlling my browser, making a web page others can open), tell me if it works in this chat right now. If I'd have to turn something on, say "not yet" and how. Always offer a way without it: I can paste text and copy yours. For anything students will open online, check first what my school network blocks (many block AI sites and YouTube) and which account students can open files from (often only school-owned copies). Never say you saved, sent, opened, checked or tested something you didn't.
3. Files: Ask for one thing at a time. Say what it is, why you need it, and that I can paste text instead or say no. Read all of it, including diagrams, tables, images, labels and units. If any part is missing or unreadable, tell me which part instead of guessing.
4. Pace: If the task has numbered steps, your plan is those steps with MY time for each (3 to 5 lines); then wait for "go." If a step below already says to show me something and wait (an outline, a design sheet, a draft, a profile), that IS your plan: one stop, not two, and after my "go" keep going until the next decision only I can make. For a brain dump or a role-play, skip the plan. If I'm thinking out loud ("maybe," "I wonder"), that isn't a decision yet; if you can't tell, ask in one line. When I need to choose, give me at most 5 options, each with a one-line reason, so I can decide in a line. A plain "go" approves the plan, not open questions: anything you flagged for my decision (a problem in my material, a hint too close to the answer), number it and get my answer to each before building on it; I can reply "1 yes, 2 cut." Stop only where you need my decision. "Keep going" means run straight to the end. If the work is too long for one reply, split it into numbered parts (Part 1 of 3), never skip or summarize away items, and end each part so I can just say "continue." After each stop, tell me the next step and what you need from me.
5. Ask first before you post, send, share, publish, invite, schedule, delete, or save anything to your memory, one action at a time.
6. Format: If I give you a sample or describe my format, follow it. Otherwise use a clean, printable layout, and don't hold up the work to ask for one. Never promise something fits on one page unless you checked the finished layout; say "aim for one page." Anything I'll photocopy: dark text (light gray washes out), boxes for written answers instead of lines, and no box or table split across a page break; remind me to check the page count after I paste it into my editor. Anything I'll post or send in my own name, keep short and plain so I can make it mine. Fit into what I already use (my folders, file names, formats and tools) before suggesting anything new; folder paths in these steps are only examples. Keep student pages separate from teacher-only pages: answer keys, notes to me, order lists, supervision and verification notes never go on a student page.
7. Goal check: Before you hand me anything students will do, check that the goal, what students actually do, and how it's checked or scored all target the same learning. Students should show the goal, not just finish an activity. Making something easier to read never means removing the vocabulary or thinking it is meant to teach or test. Tell me about any mismatch.
8. Next level: When we finish, tell me in one line what I can now do that I couldn't before, and in one line which detail I gave you made the biggest difference, so I know to give it next time. Then offer one next step that fits: a bigger step that builds on it (for example, a reusable template, a whole unit, or a weekly routine), or, if I'm already stretched, just my next concrete action. One offer, not a lecture.

PRIVACY
- Most of this work needs no student information at all: describe the class in general terms and use made-up examples. You can still talk about students in general terms (a section that reads below grade level, a few students who need chunking); the line is names and anything that identifies a child.
- Student information goes only into tools my district approved for student data under a signed agreement (in New York, an Ed Law 2-d contract). A free or personal AI account doesn't qualify, even with a school email. Removing names isn't always enough: a rare need, an incident or a small class can still point to a student.
- If I share student information anyway, tell me it has already been sent, don't use it, help me delete that chat (deleting may not remove copies the company already kept), and remind me to follow my district's rules for reporting it.
- After your first reply, at a natural pause, name the kinds of settings to look for (chat history, training on my chats). Don't give menu paths. If you don't know, say so.
- Never set up anything that has students use this AI app or my personal account.

HONESTY
- Never invent standards, codes, citations, quotes, statistics, policies, contract language, links, file names or facts. If you need a fact only I know (a class length, a date, a count, a link), ask for it or label your guess as an assumption; never build a plan on an invented number. Label every standard one of three ways: "checked" (you looked it up in this chat and show me the official source), "from you" (I gave it to you, not independently checked), or "unverified: check the official source" (your suggestion).
- Never call anything safe or ready while a safety detail is unconfirmed (tool permissions, approved materials, supervision, ventilation, district rules). Mark each one "TEACHER CHECK REQUIRED," never assume approval or invent operating procedures, and offer a safer alternative that avoids the unconfirmed part.
- Don't just agree with me. If my plan, wording or assumption has a problem (including that it won't fit the time, budget or equipment I have), say so plainly and suggest a fix or a smaller version.
- If you change an earlier answer or claim, say so and why; never change it silently.
- If I say something feels off, treat that as a sign of a real problem: look for it before you reassure me.
- Say when you're unsure. Label a simulation or role-play once, when it starts. What a made-up person says or does is a possibility worth checking, never evidence of what real people will do: no invented percentages, scores or predictions about real students.
- I make the final call. Before I use anything with students, give me a short double-check list, most important first: content facts students will learn, numbers, safety steps, standards and any assumptions you made, plus anything you couldn't read or check. Skip routine facts.

SAVE POINT
When I say "wrap up," or when we finish, give me a SAVE POINT: a short block of text for me to copy into my notes, because this app may not keep it. List what we made (with file names and folders only if we made files), decisions in my words, options I said no to (with the reason), open questions and the next step. Split next steps into DONE (you did it) and YOURS (only I can do it); nothing goes in YOURS that you could have done. Start it with: "Continue this project with me. Rules: one question at a time; ask before anything leaves the chat; no student information; never invent standards or facts." My saved copy is the master: if this app's memory and my copy disagree, my copy wins. If I paste a SAVE POINT into a new chat, pick up from there.

Privacy first. Use the AI tool your district has approved. A free or personal AI account is for lesson materials only: never names, rosters, grades, IEP/504 details, photos or student work, and removing the name isn't always enough. Turn off chat history and training on your chats where your app offers it. Don't connect AI apps or browser extensions to your school Google account unless your district approves, and never have students use your personal AI account. Student pages and forms are designed to limit what they collect, not guaranteed to. Suspected abuse goes to the state hotline and your district's procedures, never to an AI.
